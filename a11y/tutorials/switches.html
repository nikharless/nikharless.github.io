Switches

A switch is a two-state control that represents whether a setting, feature, or service is on or off. Selecting a switch generally takes effect immediately.

Switches differ from other controls:

  • Use a switch when users can immediately turn a single setting or service on or off.
  • Use a checkbox
  • Use a radio group when users must choose one option from a group of mutually exclusive choices.
  • Use a toggle button when users turn a mode, tool, or command state on or off.
  • Use a disclosure button when the control shows or hides associated content rather than changing an on/off setting.
  • Use a native <input type="checkbox"> with role="switch" whenever possible when a switch presentation is required.

WCAG requirements

  • Each switch has a programmatically determinable accessible name. (4.1.2)
  • The control exposes switch semantics. A native <input type="checkbox" role="switch"> or custom element with role="switch" provides switch semantics. (4.1.2)
  • The switch exposes aria-checked="false" when off and aria-checked="true" when on. For a native checkbox with role="switch", the native checked state is exposed automatically. (4.1.2)
  • The switch state is programmatically determinable and updates when the state changes. (4.1.2)
  • A switch does not use aria-checked="mixed". Switches have only on and off states. (4.1.2)
  • Each switch has a visible label that identifies the setting, feature, or service it controls and is associated with it programmatically. (1.3.1, 3.3.2)
  • The visible label is included in the switch's accessible name. (2.5.3)
  • The switch is operable with a keyboard. (2.1.1)
  • The switch can be reached and operated in a logical focus order. (2.4.3)
  • The switch has a visible focus indicator when it receives keyboard focus. (2.4.7)
  • The on and off states are not conveyed by color alone. (1.4.1)
  • The switch label, state indicator, and focus indicator have sufficient contrast. (1.4.3, 1.4.11)
  • The switch remains usable when text is enlarged, spacing is increased, or the viewport is narrow. (1.4.4, 1.4.10, 1.4.12)
  • The pointer target meets the minimum target-size requirement or an applicable exception applies. (2.5.8)
  • A switch that is disabled or unavailable exposes that state programmatically and does not respond as an available switch. (4.1.2)
  • If a group of switches has a common purpose, the group has an appropriate programmatic label. (1.3.1, 2.4.6)
  • If a switch controls another part of the interface, the relationship and resulting state are programmatically determinable. (1.3.1, 4.1.2)
  • If the switch affects a required form setting or preference, relevant instructions and validation errors are communicated to users. (1.3.1, 3.3.1, 3.3.2, 4.1.2)
  • Changing a switch state does not cause an unexpected change of context. (3.2.2)

Best practices

These practices improve usability and robustness, although not all are separate WCAG requirements in every implementation.

  • Use a native <input type="checkbox" role="switch"> with an associated <label> whenever possible.
  • Use a concise, stable label that names the setting or service, such as Dark mode, Notifications, or Auto-play videos.
  • Do not change the accessible name between states, such as changing Enable notifications to Disable notifications. The label identifies the setting; the on/off state communicates its current value.
  • Make the visible label clickable in addition to the switch itself.
  • Make the entire switch and label area an adequate activation target.
  • Provide a visible text or graphic indication of the on and off states in addition to a color change, such as On/Off, a changed thumb position, or a state icon.
  • If visible text indicates the state, hide duplicate On and Off text from assistive technology when the switch role and state already communicate it.
  • Use a fieldset and legend when a related group of switches has a common question or purpose.
  • Do not use a switch when the change should be reviewed, submitted, or confirmed with other form values before taking effect.
  • Do not use a switch for a selection that supports a mixed or partially selected state. Use a checkbox when a mixed state is meaningful.
  • Do not use aria-checked directly on a native checkbox with role="switch". The browser maps the checked state automatically.
  • Make immediate effects clear. If a switch starts, stops, exposes, removes, or changes significant content, provide appropriate feedback without moving focus unexpectedly.
  • Ensure on, off, disabled, hover, and focus states are visually distinguishable.
  • Do not use a switch solely as a visual style for a button, checkbox, disclosure, or another control with different semantics.

Practical test sequence

Focus Details Applicable Criteria
Identify function
  • Confirm that switch is the correct role and that the control represents an immediate on/off setting, feature, or service. (1.3.1, 4.1.2)
  • Confirm that the component is not more accurately represented as a checkbox, toggle button, radio button, disclosure button, or another control.
  • Determine whether the component uses a native checkbox with role="switch" or a custom switch implementation.
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.3.1 Info and Relationships (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (Level A)
Semantics and language
  • Confirm that the control is a native <input type="checkbox" role="switch"> where feasible, or exposes role="switch". (4.1.2)
  • Confirm each switch has a visible, descriptive label associated with it using a <label>, for/id, or an equivalent valid method. (1.3.1, 4.1.2)
  • Confirm the accessible name contains the visible label text. (2.5.3)
  • Confirm the label identifies the setting or service, rather than only its current state. (2.4.6, 4.1.2)
  • Confirm that the on or off state is exposed using the native checked state or aria-checked="true" or aria-checked="false". (4.1.2)
  • Confirm that the switch does not use aria-checked="mixed". (4.1.2)
  • For a related group, confirm that the group has an appropriate label, such as a <legend> associated with a <fieldset>. (1.3.1, 2.4.6)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.3.1 Info and Relationships (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.4.6 Headings and Labels (Level AA)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.5.3 Label in Name (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (Level A)
Screen reader
  • Confirm the control is announced as a switch. (4.1.2)
  • Verify that each switch has a meaningful accessible name that identifies the setting or service. (4.1.2)
  • Verify that the on or off state is announced on focus. (4.1.2)
  • Turn each switch on and off and confirm that the announced state updates correctly. (4.1.2)
  • If a switch is disabled, confirm that its unavailable state is announced. (4.1.2)
  • For a group of switches, confirm that the group label and relevant instructions are available to assistive technology. (1.3.1)
  • Confirm that descriptions, required information, status feedback, and error messages are associated with the switch or group when applicable. (1.3.1, 3.3.1, 4.1.2, 4.1.3)
  • When changing a switch reveals, hides, enables, disables, or changes additional content, confirm that the resulting content and relationship are understandable. (1.3.1, 4.1.2)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.3.1 Info and Relationships (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 3.3.1 Error Identification (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.3 Status Messages (Level AA)
Keyboard only
  • Use Tab and Shift+Tab to move to and away from each switch. (2.1.1, 2.1.2, 2.4.3)
  • Confirm that Space turns the switch on and off. (2.1.1)
  • Confirm that Enter toggles a button-based custom switch when that behavior is provided. A native checkbox-based switch is normally toggled with Space; Enter should not cause unexpected behavior. (2.1.1)
  • Confirm that the switch has a visible focus indicator. (2.4.7)
  • Confirm that the switch and its associated label appear in a logical focus order. (2.4.3)
  • Confirm that a disabled switch cannot be operated. (2.1.1, 4.1.2)
  • Confirm that toggling the switch does not create a keyboard trap, move focus unexpectedly, or cause an unexpected change of context. (2.1.2, 2.4.3, 3.2.2)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.1.1 Keyboard (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.1.2 No Keyboard Trap (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.4.3 Focus Order (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.4.7 Focus Visible (Level AA)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 3.2.2 On Input (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (Level A)
Immediate effects and conditional content
  • Turn the switch on and off and confirm that the expected setting, feature, or service changes immediately and predictably. (3.2.2, 4.1.2)
  • If the state change produces a status message, confirm that the result is available to assistive technology without moving focus. (4.1.3)
  • When changing a switch reveals, hides, enables, disables, or changes other content, confirm that the relationship is understandable visually and programmatically. (1.3.1, 4.1.2)
  • Confirm that newly available content appears in a logical reading and focus order. (1.3.2, 2.4.3)
  • Confirm that hidden or disabled content cannot receive keyboard focus or be operated as though it were available. (2.1.1, 2.4.3, 4.1.2)
  • Confirm that toggling the switch does not unexpectedly move focus into newly revealed content. (2.4.3, 3.2.2)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.3.1 Info and Relationships (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.3.2 Meaningful Sequence (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.1.1 Keyboard (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.4.3 Focus Order (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 3.2.2 On Input (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.3 Status Messages (Level AA)
Forms and validation
  • If the switch is part of a form, submit the form with each state selected and confirm that the submitted value is handled as intended. (4.1.2)
  • If the switch is required, submit the form with the required state not selected and confirm that the error identifies the switch or group and explains what must be done. (3.3.1, 3.3.3)
  • Confirm that required information, descriptions, and error messages are programmatically associated with the switch or group. (1.3.1, 3.3.2, 4.1.2)
  • Confirm that users can identify and correct an error without relying only on color, position, or visual styling. (1.4.1, 3.3.1)
  • Confirm that toggling the switch does not submit the form or change context unexpectedly. (3.2.2)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.3.1 Info and Relationships (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.4.1 Use of Color (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 3.2.2 On Input (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 3.3.1 Error Identification (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 3.3.3 Error Suggestion (Level AA)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (Level A)
Inspect visual aspects
  • Check contrast for the switch label, track, thumb, state indicator, and focus indicator. (1.4.3, 1.4.11)
  • Confirm that on and off states are not distinguished by color alone. (1.4.1)
  • Check the switch at 200% text resize, a 320-CSS-pixel-wide viewport, and with text-spacing overrides. The switch, label, instructions, status feedback, and error messages must remain visible, usable, and unclipped. (1.4.4, 1.4.10, 1.4.12)
  • Confirm the switch and its associated label provide an adequate pointer target. (2.5.8)
  • Confirm that the focus indicator is visible against surrounding colors and is not completely hidden by other content. (1.4.11, 2.4.11)
  • Confirm disabled styling does not make the label or state impossible to understand when users need that information. (1.4.3, 1.4.11)

  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum) (Level AA)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.4.4 Resize Text (Level AA)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.4.10 Reflow (Level AA)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast (Level AA)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.4.12 Text Spacing (Level AA)
