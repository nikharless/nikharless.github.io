Toasts

A toast is a temporary, non-modal notification that appears in response to an action or system event without moving keyboard focus. Toasts may communicate success, progress, advisory information, warnings, or errors.

Toasts differ from other notification patterns:

  • Use a toast for a brief status message that does not require an immediate user response.
  • Use a status message for non-urgent information, such as "Changes saved" or "Item added to cart."
  • Use an alert for important or time-sensitive information that requires immediate attention.
  • Use an alert dialog when users must respond before continuing, such as confirming a destructive action.
  • Use persistent inline validation or an error summary when users need to locate, understand, and correct a form error.
  • Do not use a toast as the only way to provide information that users must read, retain, act upon, or use to complete a task.

WCAG requirements

  • A toast that provides information about the success or result of an action, the waiting state of an application, the progress of a process, or the existence of an error is programmatically determinable as a status message. (4.1.3)
  • A non-urgent toast is exposed using an appropriate polite live-region implementation, such as role="status" or aria-live="polite". (4.1.3)
  • An urgent, time-sensitive toast is exposed using an appropriate assertive live-region implementation, such as role="alert" or aria-live="assertive". (4.1.3)
  • The toast is announced to assistive technology without moving keyboard focus from the user's current location. (4.1.3)
  • The message communicates the relevant status, result, progress, or error in text. (1.3.1, 4.1.3)
  • A toast that provides an error message identifies the error and makes the information available to assistive technology. (3.3.1, 4.1.3)
  • If the toast contains an action, such as Undo, Retry, View cart, or Dismiss, the action is keyboard-operable and has an accessible name. (2.1.1, 4.1.2)
  • Focusable controls within a toast appear in a logical focus order and have a visible focus indicator. (2.4.3, 2.4.7)
  • Focused toast controls are not completely hidden by the toast, sticky content, or other author-created content. (2.4.11)
  • Toast text has sufficient contrast against the toast background. (1.4.3)
  • Toast icons, close controls, focus indicators, and other meaningful non-text elements have sufficient contrast. (1.4.11)
  • The toast remains readable and usable at 200% text resize, at a 320-CSS-pixel-wide viewport, and when text-spacing overrides are applied. (1.4.4, 1.4.10, 1.4.12)
  • If toast content is removed automatically and users need time to read or operate it, users can extend, adjust, or turn off the time limit, unless a WCAG exception applies. (2.2.1)
  • A toast does not cause an unexpected change of context merely because it appears. (3.2.1)
  • Information such as success, warning, or error status is not communicated by color alone. (1.4.1)

Success and advisory toasts

A success or advisory toast provides non-urgent confirmation or status information, such as "Profile saved," "File uploaded," or "Added to favorites."

  • The toast communicates the result of an action in text. (4.1.3)
  • The toast is exposed as a non-urgent status message and is announced without moving focus. (4.1.3)
  • The toast does not interrupt the user's current screen-reader speech unnecessarily. (4.1.3)
  • If the toast disappears automatically, it remains available long enough to read or meets an applicable time-limit exception. (2.2.1)

Loading and progress toasts

A loading or progress toast communicates that a process has started, is continuing, or has completed. Examples include "Uploading file," "Search in progress," or "Report generation complete."

  • The toast communicates the relevant waiting state, progress, or completion result. (4.1.3)
  • If progress is presented as a percentage or other measurable value, the progress information is programmatically determinable. (4.1.2)
  • Progress updates are announced at useful intervals and do not interrupt users excessively. (4.1.3)
  • If a process can take a long time, users can determine that it is still running and can cancel it when cancellation is available. (4.1.3, 2.1.1)
  • Completion or failure is communicated when the process ends. (4.1.3)

Error and warning toasts

An error or warning toast communicates a problem, failed action, or condition that may require attention. Do not use a transient toast as the only way to provide errors users must correct.

  • The toast identifies the error or warning in text. (3.3.1, 4.1.3)
  • The toast is announced using an appropriate urgency level. Important or time-sensitive errors may require an assertive alert implementation. (4.1.3)
  • If users must correct an error, the error remains available through a persistent message, error summary, inline field message, or another durable method. (3.3.1)
  • When a correction is known and appropriate, the user receives a suggestion for correcting the error. (3.3.3)
  • If the toast includes Retry, View details, or another response action, the action is keyboard-operable and has an accessible name. (2.1.1, 4.1.2)
  • The toast does not rely on an error icon or red color alone to communicate that an error occurred. (1.4.1)

Actionable toasts

An actionable toast includes a control such as Undo, Retry, View details, or Dismiss. These should be used sparingly because the toast may disappear while users are navigating to its controls.

  • Each action control has an accessible name and appropriate role. (4.1.2)
  • Each action control is keyboard-operable. (2.1.1)
  • Action controls have visible focus indicators and appear in a logical focus order. (2.4.3, 2.4.7)
  • The toast remains visible long enough for users to reach and operate its actions, or users can extend, adjust, or turn off the time limit when required. (2.2.1)
  • The toast does not disappear while a user is interacting with one of its controls. (2.2.1, 2.4.3)
  • The result of an action, such as Undo or Retry, is communicated to assistive technology. (4.1.3)

Best practices

These practices improve usability and robustness, although not all are separate WCAG requirements in every implementation.

  • Use concise, specific messages, such as "Invoice downloaded" or "Address saved".
  • Use role="status" for ordinary confirmations and advisory updates.
  • Reserve role="alert" for urgent messages. Avoid interrupting users for routine confirmations.
  • Render the live-region container in the DOM before adding or changing its message content, where feasible, to improve announcement reliability.
  • Keep a toast visible long enough to read. Avoid short fixed timeouts.
  • Allow users to dismiss a toast when it remains visible, but do not automatically move focus to the close control.
  • Do not move keyboard focus to a toast that only reports status.
  • Do not put essential instructions, lengthy content, legal text, or confirmation decisions in a toast.
  • Do not put multiple unrelated controls in a toast.
  • Use a persistent notification center, inline message, dialog, or another durable location for information users may need to revisit.
  • When multiple messages may appear, queue or consolidate them to avoid overlapping visual messages and excessive screen-reader announcements.
  • Ensure a dismiss button, if provided, has an accessible name such as "Dismiss notification" or "Dismiss: Address saved".
  • Do not rely on a checkmark, warning icon, or color alone to convey the message or its severity.
  • Position toasts so they do not obscure the focused control, form error, or other content users need to operate.
  • Do not use role="alertdialog" for a standard toast. An alert dialog is modal and requires focus management.

Practical test sequence

Focus Details Applicable Criteria
Identify function
  • Confirm that the notification is a toast: it is non-modal, does not move focus, and communicates a brief status, result, progress, warning, or error. (4.1.3)
  • Determine what type of toast it is (e.g., success/advisory, loading/progress, error/warning, or actionable).
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.3 Status Messages (Level AA)
Semantics and language
  • Confirm that the toast message communicates the relevant status in text and does not rely only on color, an icon, sound, or placement. (1.4.1, 4.1.3)
  • For a success or advisory toast, confirm that it uses an appropriate status-message implementation, such as role="status" or aria-live="polite". (4.1.3)
  • For an urgent error or warning toast, confirm that it uses an appropriate assertive implementation, such as role="alert" or aria-live="assertive", when interruption is warranted. (4.1.3)
  • For an actionable toast, confirm that the controls use native buttons or links where feasible and that each control has an accessible name. (4.1.2)
  • Confirm that an error requiring correction is also available through a persistent method rather than only through the temporary toast. (3.3.1)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 1.4.1 Use of Color (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 3.3.1 Error Identification (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.3 Status Messages (Level AA)
Screen reader
  • Trigger the action that produces the toast and confirm that the toast message is announced without moving focus. (4.1.3)
  • Confirm that a routine confirmation is announced politely and does not unnecessarily interrupt typing or other screen-reader speech. (4.1.3)
  • Confirm that an important error or warning is announced with an appropriate level of urgency. (4.1.3)
  • Trigger the same toast repeatedly and confirm that updated messages are announced reliably without being repeated unnecessarily. (4.1.3)
  • For a loading or progress toast, confirm that the waiting state and completion or failure result are announced. (4.1.3)
  • For a toast with action controls, use screen-reader navigation to confirm that the controls have meaningful names, roles, and states. (4.1.2)
  • For an error toast, confirm that the error is identified and that persistent error information is available when correction is required. (3.3.1, 4.1.3)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 3.3.1 Error Identification (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.3 Status Messages (Level AA)
Keyboard only
  • Trigger a toast while keyboard focus is in a control, text field, or other active location. Confirm that the toast does not move focus. (4.1.3)
  • If the toast includes Undo, Retry, View details, Dismiss, or another action, use Tab and Shift+Tab to reach and move away from each control. (2.1.1, 2.4.3)
  • Activate each toast control with the keyboard and confirm that keyboard activation performs the same result as pointer activation. (2.1.1)
  • Confirm that each toast control has a visible focus indicator and is not completely obscured by the toast, sticky content, or another author-created element. (2.4.7, 2.4.11)
  • Confirm that the toast does not disappear while focus is on one of its controls. (2.2.1, 2.4.3)
  • Confirm that focus remains in a logical location after an action is taken or the toast is dismissed. (2.4.3)
  • Confirm that the toast does not create a keyboard trap. (2.1.2)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.2.1 Timing Adjustable (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.1.1 Keyboard (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.1.2 No Keyboard Trap (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.4.3 Focus Order (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.4.7 Focus Visible (Level AA)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum) (Level AA)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.3 Status Messages (Level AA)
Timing and persistence
  • Measure how long the toast remains visible and determine whether users need to read, retain, or operate its content. (2.2.1)
  • If the toast includes a time limit and users need more time, confirm that users can extend, adjust, or turn off the time limit unless an exception applies. (2.2.1)
  • Confirm that essential information, form errors, and consequential results remain available after the toast disappears. (3.3.1, 4.1.3)
  • Confirm that an actionable toast remains available while users are interacting with it. (2.2.1, 2.4.3)
  • Trigger several notifications in quick succession and confirm that they do not overlap, disappear before being announced, or create excessive announcements. (4.1.3)
  • For a toast with an Undo or other action control, use keyboard navigation and a screen reader where applicable to confirm that the toast remains available long enough to reach and operate the control. Do not rush; real world users may be slow to get there. If it dismisses automatically, confirm that users can extend, adjust, or turn off the time limit when required. (2.1.1, 2.2.1, 4.1.2)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 2.2.1 Timing Adjustable (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 3.3.1 Error Identification (Level A)
  • WCAG 2.2: Understanding SC 4.1.3 Status Messages (Level AA)
Inspect visual aspects
  • Check text contrast against the toast background. (1.4.3)
  • Check contrast for borders, icons, close controls, action controls, and focus indicators when they are needed to identify or operate the toast. (1.4.11)
  • Confirm that success, warning, error, and progress states are not communicated by color alone. (1.4.1)
  • Check 200% text resize, a 320-CSS-pixel-wide viewport, and text-spacing overrides. Toast text and controls must remain visible, readable, and operable without clipping, overlap, or unnecessary horizontal scrolling. (1.4.4, 1.4.10, 1.4.12)
  • Confirm that the toast does not obscure the focused control, an active form field, important page content, or another toast in a way that prevents task completion. (1.4.10, 2.4.11)
  • Confirm that any close button or action control meets the minimum target size or has sufficient spacing from adjacent targets. (2.5.8)
